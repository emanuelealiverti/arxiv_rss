Forecasting systems are refreshed at each review period, combining two tasks that need not move together: parameter estimation and model-form selection. The second is often unnecessary. This note asks when a system under a reduced-update policy should break and re-specify its form. We define specification debt as accumulated evidence against the deployed form and use it as a cost-sensitive trigger. In a closed discrete model space, the trigger is a threshold on the negative log posterior probability of the deployed form. In open production settings, the rule can use predictive score gaps, stacking weights, or calibrated diagnostics. A fixed cadence is the special case when evidence against the form accumulates at a constant rate. We apply the rule to all 47,982 monthly M4 series over an exponential smoothing grid. The results are more cautious than the rule alone would imply. Model-form choice has little average effect on accuracy. Holding the form fixed costs under one percent at the benchmark, and in a long-history subsample remains under two percent with no clear increase across horizons. A cheap fixed cadence can match full updating, and the best update frequency depends on regime. At a three-step horizon, the trigger is slightly worse than its matched fixed cadence. From six to eighteen steps, a conservative cap-eight trigger has lower average loss than a matched fixed-eight cadence under series-clustered tests. The useful signal is the out-of-sample score gap. An in-sample information-criterion analogue of specification debt does not predict realized degradation. It is complementary, modestly related to the score gap under low-noise, long-window estimation and unreliable under noise. The paper offers a decision rule for model-form maintenance and a map of where acting on it pays.
