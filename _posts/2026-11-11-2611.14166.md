The stiffness and cracking behaviour of concrete are influenced by the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), the thin shell of paste that surrounds each aggregate. Yet the ITZ's mechanical properties are poorly characterized and are usually assigned to mesoscale models through ad hoc reduction factors. Because the ITZ is only tens of micrometres thick, its stiffness must be inferred indirectly from micromechanical tests, and the resulting estimates vary widely between studies and are rarely reported with a quantified uncertainty. We present a workflow that calibrates a homogenized ITZ model against the surface displacements of several micro-cantilever bending specimens and returns its stiffness descriptors as posterior distributions rather than single values. The calibration is posed as a Bayesian inverse problem with a model-discrepancy term, sampled with a surrogate-assisted Bouncy Particle sampler, the first application of this sampler to a real-world engineering problem. We compare a pooled model, which fits one descriptor vector to all specimens, with a hierarchical model that treats the specimens as draws from a common population and is sampled with a local extension of the sampler. The hierarchical model separates the estimation uncertainty from the aggregate-to-aggregate material variability, where only the former shrinks as more data are added. Propagated through a representative volume element of several aggregates, its predictive yields broader distributions of the mesoscale stress and strain and a wider spread variety of maximum von Mises stress locations. The calibration places the ITZ thickness above the 30 {\mu}m to 80 {\mu}m commonly assumed in the literature.
