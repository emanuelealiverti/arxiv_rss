For clinical prediction models, calibration across the spectrum of predicted values is often assessed via flexible calibration curves and associated scalar summaries such as the integrated calibration index (ICI). However, these scalar summaries tend to have a finite-sample upward bias, as they are based on the absolute value of an estimate whose sampling distribution can cross zero. We show the mechanism underlying this bias and quantify its magnitude as a function of the true calibration error and its variance. This mechanism motivates easily implementable methods for reducing the bias (plug-in and inversion methods, each applied locally or globally) that are applicable to any smoothing method that generates an estimate and its sampling variance for each point on the calibration curve. These methods are general in that 1) they can be used for different outcome types (continuous, binary, time-to-event, as well as counterfactual outcomes for models that predict treatment benefit), and 2) the resulting bias-adjusted calibration error curves yield bias-corrected estimates of other metrics such as quantile-based ones (e.g., E50 and E90). Focusing on ICI as an exemplary metric, numerical and case studies demonstrate that the upward bias can be substantial, especially when the sample is small and the true ICI is close to zero. Results show that the Global Inversion method removed the most bias, particularly when the bias was substantial, although it could slightly increase the mean absolute error if the bias was small.
