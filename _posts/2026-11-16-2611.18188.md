Human communities produce technology in almost every environment, but the conditions that enable technologies in the tropics are not well understood. We test two competing hypotheses---that technological emergence is triggered by environmental stress and that technology tracks growth in effective populations---by integrating Holocene archaeological and paleoclimate evidence. We use archaeological derived radiocarbon dates from Colombia as a proxy for human activity, including dating plant management, pottery, metallurgy, and raised fields. For palaeoclimate, we use two El Ni\~no--Southern Oscillation (ENSO) reconstructions: Laguna Pallcacocha and Laguna El Junco in Ecuador. A Bayesian changepoint at 3575 cal BP (95% highest posterior density interval (HPDI) = 3811--3329) marks a ~13-fold increase in the growth rate of pre-Hispanic human activity ($r_2$/$r_1$ median = 13.3, 95% HPDI = 8.1--22.2). Plant management and pottery predate this shift by more than a millennium, whereas metallurgy and raised fields cluster within the early acceleration phase, during relative climatic and demographic stability. A Bayesian log-linear regression of human activity on ENSO frequency and intensity after the demographic changepoint reveals an asymmetric covariation: reduced ENSO frequency and intensity suppress activity by 58% and 21%, whereas ENSO frequency raises activity modestly (~11--13%). Therefore, ENSO regulates demographic activity rather than triggering technological emergence. These patterns suggest that pre-Columbian communities may have innovated when they could compute---when an effective population sat above a critical threshold and stability windows permitted combinatorial search---rather than when they were forced to by environmental stress, expressed as demographic or climatic variability.
