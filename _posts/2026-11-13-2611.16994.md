In spatial risk attribution, continuous environmental exposures and unmeasured background factors often co-vary across broad geographic scales, making it difficult to separate true exposure effects from spatial confounding using finite disease counts. Existing remedies typically project out or penalize shared spatial trends, leaving unclear whether estimation instability stems from the overall capacity of the latent random field or from its directional alignment with exposure. By decomposing the Fisher-weighted smoothing operator of an additive Poisson point process model, we show that while effective degrees of freedom measure total smoothing capacity, the relative loss of Fisher information for the exposure coefficient is governed solely by a distinct directional quantity that we call penalized collinear overlap. Rotating the spatial kernel alters this information loss while holding total capacity fixed, implying that no single smoothing scale is universally optimal. Because real-world confounding combines smooth regional trends with sharp local jumps that escape any moderate basis dictionary, point identification remains fragile. We therefore develop a partial identification framework that bounds residual confounding bias using an observable benchmark covariate. Projecting a joint spatial confidence ellipse yields valid intervals even under weak benchmarks, while incorporating a negative control outcome sharpens the bounds and flags assumption violations. Simulation studies and an analysis of childhood respiratory mortality across England demonstrate the method.
