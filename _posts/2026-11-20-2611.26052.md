Collaboration and citation networks in science are shaped by well-documented mechanisms such as preferential attachment, gender homophily, and self-citation. Whether these mechanisms operate uniformly across research areas, however, is rarely tested, because statistical network models that estimate several mechanisms jointly are difficult to apply to large bibliometric data and to compare across subnetworks. We introduce contextual relational hyperevent models (C-RHEMs), which extend relational hyperevent models by allowing network effects to vary across research topics within a single model. C-RHEMs treat each publication as one event linking its authors to its references, accommodate reference lists spanning several topics, and scale to hundreds of thousands of publications on standard hardware. In a second step, meta-analysis and meta-regression quantify heterogeneity in topic-specific effects and relate it to topic characteristics. We illustrate the approach with about 590,000 oncology publications from the PubMed Knowledge Graph, assigned to 32 OpenAlex research topics. We find that preferential attachment, gender homophily, and self-citation vary substantially across topics, with between-topic standard deviations of 30 to 70 percent of the corresponding pooled effects. Topic age, size, and gender composition account for between a third and two-thirds of this variation: older topics exhibit stronger preferential attachment, self-citation, and gender homophily, and topics with a higher share of male authors exhibit stronger self-citation. The results caution against treating network mechanisms estimated on pooled data as uniform, and C-RHEMs offer a scalable way to study how they differ across research areas.
