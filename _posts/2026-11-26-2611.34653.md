Preclinical animal studies often allow multiple valid statistical analysis strategies, which can result in different sample sizes required to achieve a target power. Because reducing animal use is an ethical, financial, and practical priority, researchers have a clear incentive to choose the strategy requiring the fewest animals. This may, however, favour an overly optimistic estimate of the sample size required, particularly when prior information is uncertain or biased. This study investigates how minimising sample size across analysis strategies affects statistical power and animal use. We conceptualise this practice as sample size hacking, describe its underlying mechanism, and evaluate its consequences using a simulation study based on a real preclinical cancer study. We varied effect sizes, the level of uncertainty in prior information, and the degree of follow-up bias to assess the resulting sample size reductions and achieved statistical power. Sample size minimisation across analysis strategies substantially reduced the achieved statistical power in several scenarios, particularly for moderate effect sizes and greater uncertainty in prior information. For larger effect sizes or in the presence of follow-up bias, the impact on power was less pronounced, but the resulting reduction in sample size was also smaller. Selecting an analysis strategy solely because it yields the smallest calculated sample size may in fact compromise statistical power, while offering only limited animal savings in other settings. These findings underscore the importance of justifying and pre-specifying the analysis strategy before sample size planning to limit potentially problematic analytical flexibility, reduce the risk of underpowered studies, and promote transparency in preclinical research.
