Whether a mortality model needs a Makeham term, the constant representing background mortality, is commonly decided with a likelihood-ratio test. The constant cannot be negative, so testing its absence places it on a boundary, where the usual chi-squared calibration fails. Assuming independent Poisson death counts and standard regularity conditions, we show that when the constant is the only parameter on a boundary the statistic converges to an equal mixture of a point mass at zero and a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom: at the 5% level the critical value is 2.71, not 3.84. We state conditions under which the correction holds for Makeham models, verify them for Gompertz--Makeham and gamma-Gompertz--Makeham, and quantify the information about the constant, which fixes the local power, the smallest detectable term, and the loss of detectability as the age window narrows. When a gamma-frailty variance is estimated at a true value of zero, the limit is no longer a chi-squared mixture and the correction rejects too often. Monte Carlo experiments show the conventional cutoff rejecting at half the nominal level, a correctly calibrated test missing a term at its detection threshold in most samples, and excess rejections persisting as exposure grows.
