Selecting a sports-ranking system is not a purely mathematical decision. It also requires agreement about what the ranking should mean and what institutional purpose it should serve. This paper distinguishes four related but different tasks in sports ranking: prediction, retrospective evaluation, championship selection, and seeding; compares the governing philosophies of major ranking families; and identifies recurring errors in ranking debates. A descriptive comparison of nine NCAA Division I sport settings illustrates how schedule density, scoring environment, cross-conference connectivity, head-to-head meetings, and common-opponent availability vary from sport to sport. The central premise is that no ranking methodology is value-free. Every system reflects choices about evidence, competitive merit, and which tradeoffs are acceptable. The paper therefore offers a structured, multi-objective framework for evaluating ranking systems according to their intended purpose/goal rather than naming one universally superior method. Its principal contribution is to make the philosophical and institutional assumptions behind ranking decisions explicit, comparable, and testable.
