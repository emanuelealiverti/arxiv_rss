Fractional-Order Hybrid SIR Forecasting of COVID-19 in Bangladesh and the United States: Memory Effects and Out-of-Sample Evaluation

medium Md Babul Hossain, Mohammad Shah Alam · stat.AP · 11 Nov 2026
The paper introduces a fractional‑order hybrid SIR model for COVID‑19 forecasting with memory effects and out‑of‑sample evaluation.

Abstract

Fractional-order epidemic models are often justified by memory, yet whether memory improves forecasts is rarely tested out of sample. We extend a hybrid SIR framework, in which a daily transmission rate is reconstructed from reported cases and then forecast, to Caputo fractional order $\alpha\in(0,1]$. We prove well-posedness, conservation, positivity and a linearized threshold at disease-free states, and derive a Gr"unwald-Letnikov reconstruction that reduces exactly to the integer-order one at $\alpha=1$. Our main theoretical result shows that for $\alpha<1$ the influence of the unknown initial infectious count vanishes, but no faster than $t^{-\alpha}$; after 332 daily steps it is about $4.4\times10^{8}$ times larger at $\alpha=0.9$ than at $\alpha=1$. In out-of-sample forecasting over 27 weekly origins, the effect of memory depends on the setting. In Bangladesh the integer order gives the lowest error for linear and constant-rate forecasts in 47 of 48 settings. In the United States, where reported cases reached about 18% of the population, a fractional order lowers the linear forecaster’s error in all 12 comparisons in 2021 but raises the constant-rate forecaster’s, with no difference significant after Holm adjustment. Against standard baselines, the SIR-based linear forecaster was more accurate than the classical SIR model and a neural network without SIR structure, and competitive with ARIMA: in the United States the fractional linear forecaster had the lowest error of all methods (seven-day MAPE 7.54% against 9.14% for ARIMA), whereas in Bangladesh ARIMA had the lowest MAPE, with no significant difference from ARIMA in either country. The choice of a fractional order should rest on out-of-sample evidence rather than in-sample fit.
