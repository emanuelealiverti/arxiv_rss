WAIVES: Weak-form Asymptotically-unbiased Instrumental-Variable Estimation for SDEs

high Hamza Ruzayqat, David M. Bortz, Vanja Dukić · stat.CO, stat.ME · 20 Nov 2026
WAIVES provides a fast, likelihood‑free weak‑form instrumental‑variable estimator for SDE parameters with asymptotic unbiasedness.

Abstract

Weak-form methods have transformed inference for deterministic dynamical systems. By integrating the governing equations against smooth, compactly supported test functions, they trade noise-amplifying differentiation for noise-attenuating integration, yielding fast and robust estimates. Extending this idea to stochastic differential equations (SDEs) has proven harder, and a consistent single-trajectory formulation in the time domain has remained out of reach. Applied naively in the time domain, the weak form makes the regressors and the errors functionals of the same noise, creating endogeneity that leaves weak-form least squares biased and inconsistent. We resolve it with instrumental variables, a classical idea from econometrics. Weak-form integrals over nearby disjoint time windows prove to be valid instruments, correlated with the regressors yet uncorrelated with the error, restoring consistency and asymptotic normality. The resulting generalized-method-of-moments estimator, WAIVES (Weak-form Asymptotically-unbiased Instrumental-Variable Estimation for SDEs), requires only a linear solve, with no likelihood evaluations, filtering, or simulation. It is therefore an order of magnitude faster than exact-likelihood methods where those exist, and it applies unchanged where they do not, with standard errors and calibrated confidence intervals supplied by the moment conditions. It also separates measurement noise from the intrinsic randomness of the dynamics. Across nine example systems, spanning linear, bistable, chaotic, excitable, state-dependent-diffusion, non-polynomial, hypoelliptic, and high-dimensional regimes (up to 120 dimensions), WAIVES recovers nonlinear drift and diffusion with negligible bias and near-nominal interval coverage. We close with two real-data illustrations: a 167-year North Atlantic sea-surface-temperature record and an optical-tweezer calibration.
