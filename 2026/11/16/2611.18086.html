Does Increasing Government Spending on Tertiary Education Provide a Return on Investment? A Bayesian Causal Inference Analysis

medium Andrew C. Parnell · stat.AP, stat.CO, stat.ME · 16 Nov 2026
Applies Bayesian causal forests to evaluate fiscal returns of higher education spending.

Abstract

When a government increases public spending on tertiary (university) education, does it provide a positive fiscal return on investment once general economic growth is separated out? Cross-country data make this hard to answer because growing economies naturally collect more tax, international ratios divide per-student spending by GDP per person and student headcount, and cohorts take years to graduate. We assemble a five-year panel of 121 countries (1985-2024) and fit continuous-treatment Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forests that model four tax changes jointly whilst adjusting for starting conditions, predicted funding, and follow-up economic growth. A +10 percentage-point (pp) increase in per-student university spending (% of GDP per person) shifts the tax mix toward direct income and profit taxation by +0.36 pp after 5 years (93% probability of a positive effect) and +0.71 pp after 10 years (96% probability). Overall tax-to-GDP takes a decade to respond as cohorts graduate: 10-year total non-resource tax rises by +0.24 pp of GDP (89% probability) and direct income tax by +0.12 pp (90% probability) per +10 pp of per-student spending, by +0.37 pp (100% probability) after five-year within-country funding increases, and by +0.22 pp in total tax (95% probability) and +0.15 pp in direct income tax (98% probability) per +25% increase in real PPP dollars per student. Comparing one entering cohort’s degree cost against its 40-year career tax payments shows a typical three- to five-fold total tax return in high-income economies (EUR 2.37 billion in Ireland returning EUR 9.66 billion in total tax with a 77% probability of net profit and EUR 2.87 billion in direct income tax, or EUR 11.55 billion in the UK returning EUR 58.33 billion), whereas expanding the university budget without protecting spending per student yields no return.
